Cutting, collision and material from one representation

Abstract

An object reconstructed by 3D Gaussian Splatting is a shell of free-floating primitives: cut it and the camera sees through to the far side, and the primitives themselves carry a colour and nothing else. FruitNinja fills the interior and supervises it against diffusion-generated cross-sections, so that a cut at an unseen angle exposes something plausible — which makes an exposed face look right without making the object into something a system can ask questions of. How many pieces exist after a cut, which primitive belongs to which, what may collide with what, what each region is made of: a point cloud answers none of these without first inventing an adjacency it does not have, and the answer then depends on the threshold used to invent it.

We keep that supervision and put the whole object — interior and skin alike — on a two-level voxel lattice: one Gaussian per occupied cell, at the cell centre, the skin refined to half the interior spacing, and every cell's attributes decoded from an eight-dimensional feature by a shared MLP. Quantising is what turns a set of points into a graph, and the graph is what the downstream questions were asking for.

Three things follow, and the same eight numbers per cell carry all of them. Cutting becomes an event with an answer: connectivity is recomputed on the grid when a plane arrives, connected-component labelling returns the piece count exactly and without a threshold, and one MPM solver is instantiated per piece, so the pieces fall and collide as distinct bodies. Material becomes a decodable property: the object separates into peel, flesh and pith without being told that a fruit has a pith, a probe on the frozen appearance feature recovers that decomposition at R² 0.851 against a shuffled control's −0.007, and feeding the per-cell moduli to the solver changes the dynamics measurably — a drop that compresses a uniform object by 4.2% compresses the decomposed one by 0.1%. And the appearance stops depending on the resolution it is rendered at: a lattice cell is sized to its own spacing rather than to the rasteriser's screen-space footprint, so a transverse section that is solid at 512 pixels is still solid at 4096 where the released models are a third background.

Nothing in the construction assumes a star-shaped object, so a genus-one object whose transverse section is an annulus trains as readily as a sphere. The representation costs an order of magnitude less than the released models — an orange is 898,563 cells and 7.2M parameters against 3,928,330 primitives and 55.0M — and interior fidelity is measured against them on held-out cuts under one protocol on one machine.

Results

Two cuts arrive while the simulation runs, and the object answers with an exact piece count and with bodies that collide. The same lattice renders the same way at every resolution we tried.

Cutting is an event with an answer

A plane arrives while the simulation runs. Two neighbouring cells stay connected iff they fall on the same side of it — one O(1) test per cell. Connected-component labelling then reports how many pieces exist: exactly, with no threshold to choose. One MPM solver is built per piece with state carried over.

The appearance is independent of the render resolution

Every primitive in the released models stores scale = −16, so exp(−16) = 1.1×10⁻⁷: they are points, and what gives them a footprint is the rasteriser's screen-space low-pass — a fixed number of pixels, not a fixed size in the world. At 512 that footprint happens to match the primitive spacing. Above it, the spacing grows with the frame and the footprint does not. A lattice cell is sized to its own spacing, so a transverse section that is solid at 512 pixels is solid at 4096.

Material, decomposed without being told

A fruit is not one material, and a filled point cloud carries a colour per primitive and no statement about what any of them is. On the lattice, the level says shell from interior and the decoded appearance says what kind of interior. Clustering those, with classes merged when their appearance centroids agree so K is not chosen by hand, separates an orange into peel, flesh, a transition and pith — nothing is told that an orange has a pith.

Arbitrary topology

The published dataset — watermelon, apple, orange, red velvet cake, loaf bread, pomegranate — is entirely genus zero and every released model is star-shaped about its centroid. We train a doughnut.

The one place topology is not free. A reference is re-mapped onto the rendered section by a bijection when the two agree in topology and by a depth profile otherwise. The reference mask fills its enclosed regions so a pale core — an orange's — stays part of the section; a doughnut's hole is also pale and also enclosed, and nothing in the image separates them. Filled, the reference reports no hole while the rendered annulus reports one, so every transverse plane takes the fallback for the whole run — a lookup on distance to the boundary, which can only draw bands parallel to the silhouette. High-frequency energy 32.3 with the test fixed against 6.3 without. And the reference saved beside it still showed crumb, so nothing in the logs looked wrong.

The reference-free metrics do not see this failure at all: CLIP scores 28.6 and 28.1 for the two runs, and cross-view consistency prefers the broken one, 0.892 against 0.861, because a surface of revolution is more self-similar across views than crumb is.

What raising the resolution would cost. Read locally, the released orange has a primitive every 0.68 pixels of a 512 render against our 1.70. Read against what the count supports over the volume, the gap is 1.63× — the difference between the two readings is clustering, their nearest-neighbour spacing being less than half their uniform spacing against our two thirds. Matching the uniform figure costs 4.3× the cells, about 3.9M, which is their primitive count. So the resolution can be raised, and doing so spends exactly the advantage in cost.

Motivation

The interior a filling method leaves behind is a set of free particles. That form answers the question the paper asks — does the exposed face look right — and it does not survive the question immediately after it. A system that cuts an object needs to know what it now holds: how many pieces exist, which primitive belongs to which, what may collide with what, what each region is made of. A point cloud answers none of these without first inventing an adjacency it does not have, and the answer then depends on the distance threshold used to invent it. Two neighbouring particles in a k-nearest-neighbour graph are connected because a number said so, not because the object is.

Why the free-particle form cannot report a piece count. There is no adjacency, so connectivity must be a k-nearest-neighbour graph with a distance threshold — and the number of pieces the system reports becomes a function of that threshold. A cut is then a filter over particles rather than an event that produces objects with identities.

The second shortfall is in the primitives themselves. Every primitive in the released models stores scale = −16: they are points with no extent in the world, and the only thing that gives them a footprint on screen is the rasteriser's low-pass, a fixed number of pixels. The models were tuned at 512, where that footprint happens to match the primitive spacing. Raise the frame and the spacing grows with it while the footprint does not, so the section a viewer was shown dissolves into the background it was covering.

A third shortfall is semantic. A filled point cloud carries a colour per primitive and no statement about what any of them is, so a simulator downstream has no material field to read and must be handed one by hand. And the published dataset is entirely genus zero — every released model is star-shaped about its centroid — so a pipeline may quietly depend on that and nobody would find out. Each of these is a property of the representation rather than of the supervision, which is why the supervision is what we keep.

Method

Pipeline

The released models are already filled, so there is no scan to start from and no density step to separate interior from exterior: the surface is recovered by binning primitives by direction from the centroid and keeping those within one fill layer of the furthest in their bin, and the paper's own internal_filling is re-run on it before quantising.

The two-level lattice

The filled interior is quantised onto a grid: one Gaussian per occupied cell, positioned at the cell centre, with the skin refined to half the interior spacing so that the surface keeps its detail without paying for a fine grid throughout. Each cell stores an eight-dimensional feature, and a single shared MLP decodes every cell's appearance and opacity from it — position is implicit in the grid index. This is the same object the fill was approximating, quantised onto a grid, and quantising is what turns a set of points into a graph.

Supervision

Only the interior trains, against diffusion-generated references for a small set of cross-sectional planes, as in the published method. The exterior is not trained at all: fourteen renders of the recovered surface are projected onto the skin cells, each cell reading the pixel it projects to weighted by cosine over a 60° cone and by the rasteriser's own coverage, and then pinned. Every training schedule we tried degraded that projected exterior, because the exterior branch averages over views and averaging removes whatever only one view sees.

Quantisation details that are not cosmetic

The grid origin must be snapped to the lattice rather than taken from the extreme primitive — taken from the furthest point, which is a skin point on the finer grid, the interior points land on half-integers where rounding merges them in pairs, and 833,401 interior points became 185,295 cells silently. And the vertical axis must be classified by extent along the principal axes rather than taken as the least-variance direction, which on a near-spherical object is assembled from sampling noise.
